Litigation Writing

Jul 26, 2026

Litigation writing — write like the best in the bar

How the best litigators actually write: Kagan’s punch, Clement’s conversational clarity, Roberts’s narrative control, the Scalia/Garner discipline. This guide is the craft layer: the rendering guide says what a document looks like and the citations guide says how every cite links; this says how to write it so a judge wants to keep reading. It applies to every register — a brief argues, a memo predicts, a blog explains — but the sentence-level craft is the same everywhere.

1. One theme, stated in one sentence

Before drafting, write the single sentence the document exists to prove — and make it a because sentence: “The motion fails because plaintiff waited eight months to claim an emergency.” If you can’t write it, you don’t understand the case yet; go back to the research. Then distill the argument to the two or three points that win, each stated with its own because. That list becomes your point headings; everything that doesn’t serve one of those points gets cut.

The theme runs through everything: the introduction states it, the fact section makes it feel inevitable, every argument heading advances it, the conclusion collects on it.

2. Point first — in the document, the section, the paragraph

  • The introduction earns its place. First paragraph: who wants what, why they should get it, in plain English. No “comes now,” no procedural throat-clearing, no quoting the standard of review before the reader knows what the fight is about.
  • Headings argue. A point heading is a complete contention the judge could adopt as a holding: “The complaint pleads no facts connecting Acme to the alleged scheme” — never a label like “The Fraud Claim” or “Legal Standard Applied.”
  • Paragraphs lead with their conclusion. The first sentence of every paragraph carries its point; the rest supports it. A judge skimming only first sentences should get the whole argument. CRAC discipline per issue: conclusion, rule, application, counterargument answered, conclusion again — but written as flowing prose, never as labeled parts.
  • Paragraphs, not bullets. Analysis is topic sentences, crisp rule statements, and reasoning in prose. Lists only for genuinely enumerable items (elements, a sequence of filings) — never bullet-point “analysis.”

3. Sentences: short, plain, varied

The modern register — Kagan, Clement, the plain-English school — is conversational confidence.

  • Cut the lawyerisms. Not “in this matter” but here. Not “subsequently” but later. Not “demonstrated” but shown. Not “due to the fact that” but because. Not “purchased” but bought. Not “in the absence of” but without. Not “prior to” but before. Never “aforementioned,” “hereinafter,” “said contract,” “such [noun],” “pursuant to” (use under).
  • Open punchy. Kagan starts sentences with one-syllable words that push the prose forward: So. But. And. That. Here. Now. Starting with “But” or “And” is correct and powerful.
  • Vary the rhythm. Follow a long sentence with a three-word one. That lands. Use a dash for emphasis, a colon to set up a payoff. A page of uniform 25-word sentences is a sedative.
  • Strong verbs, no padding. “The court rejected the argument,” not “the argument was found by the court to be unpersuasive.” Pick the occasional vivid verb — thwart, hoodwink, sidestep, gut — where it’s earned; one zinger per page persuades, five per page perform.
  • Adverbs and intensifiers are confessions. “Clearly,” “plainly,” “it is beyond dispute” — delete them; if it were clear you wouldn’t need the adverb. Show the strength, don’t assert it.

4. Quotes: weave snippets, never dump blocks

Scalia and Garner: “A remarkably large number of lawyers seem to believe that their briefs are improved if each thought is expressed in the words of a governing case. The contrary is true.”

  • Weave short fragments into your own sentence. The Olson/Boies model — three quoted phrases threaded through one sentence of their own prose: the disadvantage “‘is born of’ nothing more than naked ‘animosity.’” The quoted words carry the court’s authority; the sentence stays yours, and the cite attaches naturally.
  • Quote the operative seven words, paraphrase the rest. If a passage runs longer than a line, the quotable core is shorter than you think. A block quote is a signal the writer didn’t do the work — judges skip them.
  • Never alter a quote silently. Brackets and ellipses for edits; the words stay verbatim (and tool-verified — never quoted from memory).

5. Analogies and concrete images

Abstract doctrine persuades nobody; a concrete image does. Ginsburg, dissenting in Shelby County: throwing out preclearance because it has worked “is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet.” Roberts built whole arguments on homely comparisons. The recipe: take the doctrinal move you’re attacking or defending, find the everyday transaction with the same logic, state it in one sentence, move on. One good analogy per document; it should clarify, not decorate. Same with hypotheticals: “On plaintiff’s theory, a driver who honks once has ‘harassed’ — that cannot be the rule.”

6. Let the best cases win for you

Great briefs are won in the research, then written so the authority does the heavy lifting:

  • Fewer, better cases. One binding, on-point, well-treated case beats a string cite of five. String cites read as insecurity. Lead with the controlling holding; add at most one reinforcing authority with a parenthetical that earns its place.
  • Synthesize rules; don’t write book reports. Never march through cases one paragraph each (“In Smith… In Jones… In Brown…”). State the rule the cases add up to, in your words, then cite them for it. The case discussion exists to serve your rule statement, not the reverse.
  • Parentheticals do work. “(affirming dismissal where plaintiff waited six months)” — a participial phrase that proves the cite is on point. If you can’t write the parenthetical, reconsider the cite.
  • Argue from the case’s facts when they’re better than its language. “The Winter plaintiff at least alleged imminent harm; here, plaintiff alleges none” persuades more than another quote.

7. Confront adverse authority — head-on, early, on your terms

Take the other side’s argument at its strongest: state its best form fairly in one sentence, then beat it. Never ignore a case the other side will lead with — the judge will find it, and your silence will read as concession. Distinguish on the facts, the posture, or the rule’s own limits; if it’s genuinely against you, say why it’s wrong or why it doesn’t control, candidly. In an objective memo this is mandatory even-handedness; in a brief it’s inoculation — you choose the frame the bad case is seen through. Burying it in a footnote is the worst of both.

8. Facts that persuade without arguing

The statement of facts wins cases before the argument starts — by selection and arrangement, never by adjectives. Show, don’t tell: “Acme shipped the product three weeks after its own lab flagged the defect” needs no “egregiously.” Tell it as a story with people doing things, in chronological order unless another order serves the theme; front-load the facts your argument will need, and include the bad fact you’ll have to deal with — framed, not hidden. Every sentence cites the record.

9. Before and after

Before: It is respectfully submitted that the aforementioned Motion should be denied due to the fact that Plaintiff has failed to adequately demonstrate the existence of irreparable harm, as is clearly required under the applicable legal standard set forth in the relevant case law.

After: Plaintiff waited eight months to claim an emergency. That delay defeats the motion: an injunction requires harm that is “likely,” not speculative — and a party facing real irreparable harm does not wait two-thirds of a year to say so.

Before: In Smith v. Jones, the court held that delay was a factor. In Brown v. Davis, the court also considered delay. In Green v. White, the court found that a six-month delay weighed against the plaintiff.

After: Courts in this circuit treat unexplained delay as evidence the claimed harm is not irreparable — a party facing imminent injury “does not stand idly by.” [linked cite] (affirming denial after a six-month delay).

Before: Defendant’s argument is wholly without merit and should be summarily rejected.

After: Defendant reads the statute to cover only written contracts. The statute says “any agreement.” Those words decide this.

10. Register: same craft, different posture

Deliverable Posture What changes
Brief / motion Argues Theme-driven; headings are contentions; adverse authority confronted and distinguished; confident, never shrill.
Memo Predicts Same point-first craft, neutral verbs (“a court would likely hold”); both sides get their best statement; candor over advocacy.
Blog / client alert Explains Plain-English, terms of art defined inline; engaging but never breathless; balanced — the firm’s public credibility is the product.
Summary / work product Records Even-handed, faithful to the record, zero spin; the reader must be able to rely on it blind.

The constants across all four: point first, short sentences, plain words, woven quotes, linked authority, no filler, and honesty about what the law actually says. And in every register: no emojis, no marketing tone — plain, professional, restrained, written for a busy attorney who skims first. When in doubt, shorter.
